Hong Bang Dynasty Era

Kinh Duong Vuong
[1b] His taboo name was Loc Tuc, a descendant of the Than Nong clan.
Year of Nham Tuat, 17th year. In the past, De Minh, the third-generation descendant of Viem De of the Than Nong clan, fathered De Nghi. Later, during a tour of the South, De Minh reached Ngu Linh, married the daughter of Vu Tien, and fathered the king [Kinh Duong Vuong]. The king was a man of sagacious intelligence; De Minh loved him dearly and wanted him to succeed to the throne. The king persistently yielded to his older brother and dared not obey the command. De Minh then established De Nghi as the heir to rule the North, and enfeoffed the king as Kinh Duong Vuong to rule the South, calling it the kingdom of Xich Quy.
The king married the daughter of Dong Dinh Quan named Than Long, and fathered Lac Long Quan (Note: The Tang annals record: during the Kinh Duong period, there was a goat-herding woman who claimed to be the youngest daughter of Dong Dinh Quan; she married the second son of Kinh Xuyen but was abandoned, and wrote a letter asking Lieu Nghi to report this to Dong Dinh Quan. Thus, Kinh Xuyen and Dong Dinh had been allied by marriage for generations).

Lac Long Quan
[2a] His taboo name was Sung Lam, son of Kinh Duong Vuong.
The king married De Lai's daughter, Au Co, and fathered a hundred sons (legend has it she gave birth to a hundred eggs), who are the ancestors of the Bach Viet. One day, the king said to Au Co: "I am of the dragon race, you are of the fairy race; water and fire are incompatible, it is truly difficult for us to be together." They then bid each other farewell, dividing 50 children to follow their mother to the mountains, and 50 children to follow their father to the South (some versions record to the South Sea), and enfeoffed the eldest son as Hung Vuong to succeed the throne.
Historian Ngo Si Lien said: When heaven and earth were first formed, there was a being created from vital energy, which was the Pan Gu clan. From the manifestation of energy came the manifestation of physical forms; nothing exists outside the two energies of yin and yang. The I Ching says: "Heaven and earth intermingle, all things are transformed and purified; male and female unite their essences, all things are born." Therefore, there is husband and wife first, then father and son, and after father and son comes ruler and subject. [2b] However, the birth of sages and worthies is always extraordinary, which is due to the mandate of heaven. Swallowing a dark bird's egg to give birth to the Shang dynasty, stepping on a giant's footprint to establish the Zhou dynasty, are all recorded facts of this nature. The descendant of the Than Nong clan, De Minh, married the daughter of Vu Tien and fathered Kinh Duong Vuong, who is the founding ancestor of the Bach Viet. The king married the daughter of Than Long and fathered Lac Long Quan; Lac Long Quan married the daughter of De Lai and had the auspicious blessing of fathering a hundred sons. Is that not what laid the foundation for our Viet nation's enterprise? Examining the Tongjian Waiji (Comprehensive Mirror, Outer Annals), it states: De Lai was the son of De Nghi; according to that record, Kinh Duong Vuong was the biological younger brother of De Nghi, yet they intermarried; perhaps it was because that era was still primitive and rites and music had not yet been established?

Hung Vuong
[3a] The son of Lac Long Quan (taboo name unknown), established the capital in Phong Chau (now Bach Hac district).
Upon ascending the throne, Hung Vuong set the national name as Van Lang (this country bordered the South Sea to the east, Ba Thuc to the west, Dong Dinh lake to the north, and Ho Ton - which is Champa, now Quang Nam - to the south). He divided the country into 15 administrative regions: Giao Chi, Chu Dien, Vu Ninh, Phuc Loc, Viet Thuong, Ninh Hai, Duong Tuyen, Luc Hai, Vu Dinh, Hoai Hoan, Cuu Chan, Binh Van, Tan Hung, Cuu Duc; all were vassal lands of Hung Vuong; while the region called Van Lang was where the king established the capital. He appointed civil generals called Lac Hau, and military generals called Lac Tuong. The king's sons were called Quan Lang, and daughters were called Mi Nuong. Officials in charge of affairs were called Bo Chinh; their hereditary succession from father to son was called phu dao. Kings of all generations were called Hung Vuong. At that time, the people living in the forests and mountains saw that the rivers and streams had shrimp and fish, so they invited each other to catch fish for food, but were often harmed by water monsters (thuong luong). [3b] They came to report this to the king. The king said: "The barbarians in the mountains are different from aquatic species; those aquatic species favor their own kind and hate other species, which is why you are harmed by them." The king then told everyone to use ink to tattoo images of water monsters on their bodies. From then on, the water monsters saw them and no longer bit or harmed them. The tattooing custom of the Bach Viet people probably started from there.

During the reign of the 6th Hung King, in Phu Dong village, Vu Ninh region, there was a wealthy family who had a son. By the time he was over three years old, he ate well and grew big but did not know how to speak or laugh. At a time when there was urgent news of danger in the country, the king sent envoys to find someone who could repel the enemy. On that day, the child suddenly spoke, telling his mother to invite the royal envoy in, and said: "Please grant me a sword and a horse, the king needs not worry about anything." The king granted him a sword and a horse; the child immediately rode the horse, brandished the sword, and charged forward with the royal army following behind, defeating the enemy troops at the foot of Vu Ninh mountain. The enemy troops turned their spears against each other, with many casualties; the survivors all bowed down, revered the child as a heavenly general, and immediately surrendered. The child rode his horse [4a] into the sky and departed. The king ordered the child's home garden to be renovated into a temple for annual sacrifices. Later, Ly Thai To granted him the title Xung Thien Than Vuong. (The temple is located next to Kien So pagoda, Phu Dong village).

During the reign of King Cheng of Zhou [1063-1026 BC], our country of Viet visited the Zhou dynasty for the first time (it is unclear during which Hung King's reign), claiming to be the Viet Thuong clan, and offered a white pheasant. The Duke of Zhou said: "If our political decrees do not reach them, a gentleman does not treat them as his subjects," and then ordered the construction of a south-pointing chariot to escort the envoys back to their country.

At the end of the Hung Vuong era, the king had a daughter named Mi Nuong, who was remarkably beautiful. Thuc Vuong heard of her and came to propose marriage. The king wanted to agree, but the Hung Hau advised against it: "They want to conquer our country and are only using marriage as an excuse." Because of this, Thuc Vuong harbored resentment. The king, wanting to find a worthy person to marry her to, told his subjects: "This daughter is of fairy descent; only someone with sufficient talent and virtue can be my son-in-law." At that time, two men arrived from outside and bowed in the courtyard to propose. The king found it strange; when asked, they replied that one was [4b] Son Tinh (Mountain Spirit) and the other was Thuy Tinh (Water Spirit), both from the great realm, who had heard the king had a divine daughter and made bold to come request her hand. The king said: "I have only one daughter, how can I have both of you as worthy sons-in-law?" He then set a condition that the next day, whoever brought sufficient betrothal gifts first would marry her. The two men obeyed, bowed in gratitude, and left. The next day, Son Tinh brought jewels, gold, silver, mountain birds, and forest beasts as gifts. The king kept his promise and married his daughter to him. Son Tinh took his wife to live on a high peak of Tan Vien mountain. Thuy Tinh also arrived with betrothal gifts later; furious and regretful that he was too late, he summoned clouds and rain, raised the waters to flood the land, and led aquatic creatures in pursuit. The king and Son Tinh stretched iron nets across the upper reaches of the Tu Liem river to block them. Thuy Tinh took another river, from Ly Nhan to the foot of Quang Oai mountain, then followed the banks up to the Hat river estuary, out to the great river, and turned into the Da river to attack Tan Vien. Everywhere he went, he dug deep abysses and swamps, storing water to plot a surprise attack. Son Tinh possessed divine powers of transformation; he called [5a] the barbarians to weave bamboo into water barriers, and used crossbows to shoot downwards. The scaled and shelled creatures hit by arrows all fled. Ultimately, Thuy Tinh could not invade Tan Vien mountain. (Legend has it that from then on, Son Tinh and Thuy Tinh harbored a generational feud, and every year during the high water season, they often fought each other).

Tan Vien mountain is a high mountain range in our Viet country, and its sacredness is highly responsive. After Mi Nuong married Son Tinh, Thuc Vuong was furious and instructed his descendants to destroy Van Lang and seize the country. In the time of his descendant, Thuc Phan, who possessed courage and strategy, he attacked and conquered the country.

Historian Ngo Si Lien said: During the Hung Vuong era, feudal lords were established to act as a defensive screen, and the country was divided into 15 regions. In those 15 regions, there were chiefs and deputies. The king assigned his younger sons in hierarchical order to govern them. As for saying 50 children followed their mother to the mountains, how do we know it wasn't like this? Since the mother acted as the supreme leader, the children all became lords of a territory. Just look at the tribal chiefs of the barbarians today who call themselves male phu dao, [5b] female phu dao. As for the story of Son Tinh and Thuy Tinh, it is highly bizarre; believing completely in books is worse than having no books at all, so let us temporarily recount the old story to pass down the doubt).

The above is the Hong Bang dynasty era, starting from Kinh Duong Vuong being enfeoffed in the year of Nham Tuat, concurrent with De Nghi, and passing down to the end of the Hung Vuong era, equivalent to the 57th year of King Nan of Zhou [258 BC], which was the year of Quy Mao, totaling 2,622 years [2879 - 258 BC].
